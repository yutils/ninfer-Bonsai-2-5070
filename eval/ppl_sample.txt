The history of computing is, in large part, the history of finding better representations
for the same underlying information. A number can be written in binary, in decimal, or in
hexadecimal, and each of these choices carries a different cost in storage while preserving
exactly the same value. The same principle applies to the weights of a neural network. A
matrix of thirty thousand rows and four thousand columns occupies a fixed amount of space
when stored as thirty-two bit floating point numbers, but the amount of information that
matrix actually carries is usually far smaller than that raw footprint suggests.

Quantization is the practice of exploiting that gap. Instead of storing every weight with
full precision, we store a low precision code plus enough side information to reconstruct
an approximation of the original value. The simplest schemes use a single scale factor for
an entire tensor. More refined schemes divide the tensor into groups and give each group its
own scale, which costs a few extra bytes but dramatically reduces the reconstruction error
when the distribution of values varies across the tensor.

Ternary quantization pushes this idea to an extreme. Each weight is constrained to one of
only three values: negative one, zero, or positive one. Such a weight carries log base two
of three bits of information, which is approximately one point five eight bits, and that
number is the one usually quoted when people describe these models. The storage cost is
slightly different, because the codes are packed two per byte and each group of one hundred
and twenty eight weights carries a half precision scale. When both contributions are counted
the effective cost is about two point one two five bits per weight.

The surprising result is that models survive this treatment. A network trained or post
processed to operate with ternary weights loses some accuracy, but far less than the
apparent severity of the compression would suggest. The reason is that the redundancy in
trained weights is enormous. Most of what a trained matrix encodes is a coarse pattern of
which directions matter, and that pattern survives being rounded to three levels.

A Hadamard transform is an orthogonal change of basis built entirely from plus and minus
one entries. Applying it costs almost nothing, because it can be computed with additions
and subtractions alone, and it has the useful property of spreading the energy of a vector
evenly across all of its coordinates. When the weights of a layer are heavily concentrated
in a few directions, quantizing them directly produces large errors. Rotating the weight
matrix into the Hadamard basis first spreads that concentration out, so that no single
coordinate dominates, and the subsequent rounding error becomes much more uniform and much
smaller in magnitude.

This is why the rotation and the quantization are designed together rather than separately.
The rotation is folded into the surrounding computation so that it costs nothing at
inference time beyond a small number of extra additions on the activations, while the
weights themselves stay in their compact ternary form. The result is a model that reads
roughly half as many bytes per token as a four bit quantized model of the same shape.

On a bandwidth limited device, that difference is the whole story. Decoding a single token
from a dense transformer requires touching every weight in the model exactly once, so the
time per token is bounded below by the size of the weights divided by the memory bandwidth
of the device. Cutting the weight traffic in half nearly doubles that bound. This is the
practical argument for ternary weights, and it is also the reason the measurement that
matters is not the nominal bit width but the achieved tokens per second on real hardware.

Memory bandwidth itself has a peculiar economics. A modern consumer graphics card can
perform an enormous number of arithmetic operations per second, but it can only move a
limited number of bytes from its memory into its cores in the same interval. For small
batch sizes, which is the normal case for interactive inference, the arithmetic units sit
mostly idle while the memory system is saturated. Any optimization that reduces bytes moved
therefore translates directly into throughput, while an optimization that only reduces
arithmetic may produce no measurable improvement at all.

Speculative decoding attacks the same problem from a different direction. Instead of
reducing the bytes needed for one token, it tries to produce several tokens for roughly the
cost of one. A small draft model proposes a short sequence of candidate tokens, and the
large model then verifies all of them in a single forward pass. Because the verification
step reads the weights only once regardless of how many candidates are being checked, the
candidates that the large model accepts are obtained almost for free. The tokens that are
rejected cost a small amount of wasted work, so the net benefit depends on the acceptance
rate, which in turn depends on how closely the draft model tracks the large one.

When the draft head is part of the same model rather than a separate network, the proposal
quality tends to be high, because the draft has access to the same internal representation
that the main head uses. This arrangement is often called multi token prediction, and it
typically accepts between half and three quarters of the proposed tokens. At an acceptance
rate of seventy five percent with a draft length of three, a single verification round
yields on average two and a quarter tokens, which is a substantial improvement over the one
token per round that a naive decoder achieves.

Both techniques compose cleanly. Ternary weights reduce the cost of every verification
round, and speculative decoding reduces the number of rounds needed to produce a given
number of tokens. A model that uses both can therefore be several times faster than a
baseline that uses neither, without any change to the underlying mathematics of the
transformer architecture itself.

The transformer architecture has remained remarkably stable through all of this. An input
sequence is embedded into a vector space, a series of attention and feed forward blocks
refine that representation, and a final projection produces a distribution over the
vocabulary. Attention allows every position to gather information from every other position
that precedes it, weighted by a compatibility score computed from queries and keys. The
feed forward blocks then process each position independently. This division of labour,
mixing across positions and then processing within a position, turns out to be enough to
support very sophisticated behaviour when the model is large enough and trained on enough
data.

During generation, the results of the attention computation for previously processed tokens
are stored in a cache so that they do not need to be recomputed. This cache grows linearly
with the length of the context, and on a device with limited memory it eventually becomes
the binding constraint. Storing the cached keys and values at reduced precision extends the
reachable context length substantially, at the cost of a small amount of additional error
in the attention scores. Measuring whether that error matters is an empirical question, and
the usual way to answer it is to place a known fact somewhere in a long document and check
whether the model can still retrieve it.

Evaluation of a compressed model therefore has two distinct parts. The first is numerical:
does the model still assign high probability to text it should find predictable, which is
measured by perplexity on a fixed corpus. The second is behavioural: does the model still
produce coherent, useful output, and does it still retrieve information from long contexts.
A compression scheme that passes the first test but fails the second is not useful in
practice, and a scheme that passes the second but fails the first has usually just been
evaluated on an easy task.

These two tests are also the ones most likely to reveal a mistake in the implementation
rather than in the idea. A packing error that swaps two bit fields, or a scale that is read
from the wrong offset, will usually leave the model able to produce fluent looking text
while destroying its numerical accuracy almost completely. The perplexity number collapses
from a small value to something enormous, and that collapse is the signal that the format
is being interpreted incorrectly somewhere in the pipeline. Conversely, a model that is
numerically sound but produces repetitive nonsense usually has a bug in the attention mask
or in the position handling rather than in the weight format.

Getting all of this right is mostly a matter of being careful about definitions. A group
size, an alignment requirement, a byte order, and the exact convention used for the sign
of a zero weight are all places where two implementations can disagree while both appearing
to work. The only reliable way to check is to compare against a reference on real data and
to insist that the numbers match, not merely that the output looks reasonable.
